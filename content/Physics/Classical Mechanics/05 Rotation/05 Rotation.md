## Overview

Rotational motion extends the concepts of linear motion to [[System|Systems]] that rotate about an axis. Instead of describing motion using position, [[Velocity]], and [[Acceleration]], rotational motion uses angular quantities such as [[Angular Position]], [[Angular Velocity]], and [[Angular Acceleration]].

Just as mass resists changes in linear motion, the resistance to rotational motion is described by the [[Moment Of Inertia]], which depends on how mass is distributed relative to the axis of rotation.

The rotational equivalent of [[Force]] is [[Torque]], which measures how effectively a force causes rotation. Torque depends on both the magnitude of the force and its distance from the axis. The relationship between torque and motion is given by [[Newton’s Second Law For Rotation]], which states that the net torque is equal to the moment of inertia times the angular acceleration.

Rotational motion can also be described using [[Energy]]. A rotating object possesses [[Rotational Kinetic Energy]], which depends on its moment of inertia and angular velocity.

Another key concept is [[Angular Momentum]], which is the rotational analog of [[Linear Momentum]]. For a single particle, this is described by [[Angular Momentum Of A Particle]], while for extended objects it is given by [[Angular Momentum Of A Rigid Body]]. In the absence of external torque, angular momentum is conserved, as described by the [[Conservation Of Angular Momentum]].

To simplify calculations involving complex objects, tools such as the [[Parallel Axis Theorem]] and [[Perpendicular Axis Theorem]] are used to determine the moment of inertia about different axes.

Finally, motion under constant angular acceleration can be analyzed using the [[Rotational Kinematic Equations]], which are direct analogs of the linear kinematic equations.

Together, these concepts provide a complete framework for analyzing rotational systems and understanding how forces produce rotational motion.
