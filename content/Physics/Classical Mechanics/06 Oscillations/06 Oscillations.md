## Overview

Oscillatory motion describes [[System|Systems]] that move back and forth around an [[Equilibrium]] position. This type of motion is governed by a [[Restoring Force]], which always acts to return the system toward equilibrium.

The simplest form of oscillatory motion is [[Simple Harmonic Motion]], in which the restoring [[Force]] is proportional to [[Displacement]] and directed opposite to it. This leads to sinusoidal motion described by the [[SHM Equations Of Motion]].

In oscillatory systems, motion is characterized by quantities such as [[Amplitude]], [[Phase]], and [[Angular Frequency]]. The [[Period And Frequency]] describe how long each cycle takes and how often oscillations occur.

A common example is the [[Mass Spring System]], where the restoring force is provided by the spring. Another important system is the [[Simple Pendulum]], which exhibits oscillatory motion and can be approximated as simple harmonic motion under the [[Small Angle Approximation]]. More generally, the [[Physical Pendulum]] describes oscillations of extended rigid bodies.

[[Energy]] plays a central role in oscillatory motion. In ideal systems, energy continuously transfers between [[Kinetic Energy|Kinetic]] and [[poten]] forms, as described in [[Energy In Oscillatory Motion]]. The total energy remains constant in the absence of non-conservative effects.

In real systems, oscillations are affected by [[Damping]], which gradually reduces amplitude over time, leading to [[Damped Oscillations]]. When an external periodic force is applied, the system undergoes [[Driven Oscillations]], which can lead to [[Resonance]] when the driving frequency matches the natural frequency.

The sharpness and strength of resonance are characterized by the [[Quality Factor]], which describes how efficiently a system stores energy.

Together, these concepts provide a complete framework for understanding periodic motion, energy exchange, and the behavior of real oscillating systems.
